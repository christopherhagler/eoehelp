Identity study · eoehelp.org

Four marks for a disease you can see

EoE has an unusually literal visual signature. Anyone who has had an endoscopy has seen their own esophagus on a screen — concentric rings, furrows, a narrowed channel. That gives this identity something most health brands don't have: a form the audience already recognizes from their own medical records.

Each direction below is shown at the sizes it actually has to survive, on the surfaces it actually has to sit on. Nothing here is decorative — a mark that only works at 200px is not a logo, it's an illustration.

Favicon
16px. Must stay legible as a browser-tab silhouette, next to thirty other tabs.
App masthead
24px beside the wordmark, on white and on the dark theme.
Clinical report
Monochrome, printed. A gastroenterologist may read it on paper.
Tone
Credible to a specialist, not clinical to the point of cold. Never cute.
01

Rings

Recommended

The endoscopic signature of EoE, drawn as three rings that open.

Concentric rings — trachealization — are what an EoE esophagus looks like through a scope. Patients recognize this shape from their own procedure photos, which is a rare thing for a logo to be able to do.

The rings are deliberately broken, and the gaps widen as they move inward, opening upward into a channel. So the mark reads twice: the disease as it appears on a screen, and the passage clearing. That second reading is the product's whole promise, and it keeps the mark from being a diagram of an illness.

The risk: concentric circles are also a target, a radar, a record button. The offset gaps are what pull it away from that — if the rings were closed, this would be generic. Worth testing against a few patients before committing.

02

Passage

Strong alternative

The esophagus in profile — narrowed at the middle, opening below.

Two mirrored curves form a channel that pinches at the midpoint and opens out below it. It is the stricture and the relief of it in a single gesture, and it is abstract enough to work as a mark without needing to be explained.

This is the quieter, more timeless of the two lead directions. It reads as a considered piece of geometry first and a medical reference second — which may age better than Rings if the product ever widens beyond EoE.

03

Fourteen days

Product-first

Not the disease — the daily log, and the trend going down.

A descending run of bars: a symptom score falling across a scoring window. This direction deliberately refuses to depict the illness at all, and points at what the product does instead — you log every day, and the line comes down.

It is the most optimistic of the four and the easiest to explain to someone who has never had an endoscopy. It is also the one a clinician would read fastest, because a falling DSQ score is exactly what they are looking for.

04

Aperture

Most durable at 16px

Looking down the scope: one open ring, one fixed point.

A single ring, broken at the upper right, around a solid centre. It is the view down an endoscope reduced to its simplest possible statement, and the break keeps it from closing into a plain circle.

Technically this is the strongest of the four: the heaviest silhouette, the fewest strokes, and the only one that is unambiguously itself at 16 pixels. If favicon legibility is the deciding constraint, this wins on merit.

Where I'd land

Rings, with Aperture held in reserve as the favicon if Rings proves too fine at 16px. Rings is the only one of the four that says something true and specific about this disease, and the widening gaps keep it from being a picture of illness — it shows the passage opening, which is the thing patients are actually trying to get back.

Passage is the more elegant object and would be the safer pick for a broader health brand. It's the one to choose if you ever expect to cover more than EoE.
